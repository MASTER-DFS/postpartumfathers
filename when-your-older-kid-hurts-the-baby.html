When the baby gets hurt

When your older kid hurts the baby

It happens fast. A ride-on toy rolls over the baby’s hand, a hug turns into a squeeze, or a shove sends the baby tumbling. Sometimes it’s an accident and sometimes it isn’t. Either way your heart is pounding. Here’s what to do first, and how to get both kids, and you, through it.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Right now

  • Check the baby first. Get to them and look them over.
  • Call 911 or go to the ER if the baby is hard to wake, throws up more than once, won’t move an arm or leg, or anything looks wrong.
  • Breathe before you talk to your older one. Calm first, teaching later.

First, check the baby

Get to the baby and look them over. If they’re awake and crying, it’s fine to pick them up and comfort them. If they aren’t moving or won’t wake, call 911 and leave them where they are unless they’re in danger. Most of the time it ends with a scared baby who settles in your arms. But a baby can’t tell you what hurts, so the bar for getting help is lower than it would be for an older kid.

Call 911 or go to the ER right away if the baby:

  • Is hard to wake, or seems very sleepy or floppy
  • Passed out, even for a moment, or has a seizure
  • Throws up more than once
  • Won’t move an arm or leg, or screams when you touch it
  • Can’t be settled no matter what you try, or won’t feed
  • Has one pupil bigger than the other, or bleeding that won’t stop with pressure
  • Just doesn’t seem like themselves, or anything looks wrong to you

If none of that is happening but you’re not sure, call your pediatrician’s office. Most have a nurse line or an on-call doctor after hours, and the number is usually on the office voicemail. If the baby bumped their head and is under two, call the same day even if they seem fine. Keep a close eye on them for the next day too, because some signs show up later.

You are not overreacting by calling. It’s a short call that either puts your mind at rest or catches something early.

One thing to be absolute about: if you ever think a baby may have been shaken, by anyone, call 911 straight away. Shaking can cause serious harm that doesn’t show on the outside.

Your own fear and anger

This moment hits hard. Fear for the baby and anger at your older one can arrive in the same second, about two kids you love. Your heart pounds and your voice wants to go loud. That’s your body trying to protect your baby, and it’s normal.

The order that helps: baby safe in your arms or the crib, older one in a safe spot nearby, then a few slow breaths before you say anything. Don’t grab them hard or yell in their face. If you already did, you can fix it later: “I got scared and I yelled. I’m sorry. I love you.” Kids learn a lot from a dad who says that.

Accident or on purpose

Lots of these are accidents. A ride-on toy rolls over a hand, a hug turns into a squeeze, a toy gets thrown and lands wrong, or a “helpful” lift goes badly. Little kids don’t yet understand how big and strong they are next to a baby.

Some are on purpose: a shove, a smack, a bite. That doesn’t mean you have a mean kid. Jealousy is normal when a new baby takes up so much of you, and a toddler or preschooler can’t say “I miss you,” so it comes out through their hands. Either way, your older one is a little kid, not a bad one.

Handling your older one, without shame

  • Separate and calm first, teach after. Nobody learns anything while everyone is upset, you included.
  • Keep it short and plain. “Ouch. The baby got hurt. We are gentle with the baby.” Then stop.
  • Skip the shame. Don’t call them bad, and don’t tell them they could have killed the baby. That scares them without teaching them anything.
  • If it was an accident, give them a way to help: “Can you bring the baby’s blanket?” Helping fixes the bad feeling.
  • If it was on purpose, name the feeling: “You’re mad that the baby gets so much of me.” Keep any consequence small and right away, like ending that game.
  • Later, when everyone is calm, practice gentle touch together on a doll or stuffed animal, or on the baby’s feet with your hand over theirs.
  • Give them some time that’s just theirs. A few minutes a day, phone down, their choice. It does more than any punishment.

For more on jealousy, hitting and going backwards after a new baby, see when your older kid acts out.

Making accidents less likely

  • Don’t leave them alone together yet, even for a minute. Not as a punishment, just as the setup. A playpen, a gate, or the baby in your arms takes care of most of it.
  • Keep ride-on toys, balls and big toys away from where the baby lies. Floor time for the baby works best on a mat your older one knows is the baby’s spot.
  • Only grown-ups lift or carry the baby. Your older one can hold the baby sitting down, with you right there.
  • Pick up small toys. Your older one’s small pieces can be a choking risk once the baby starts grabbing and mouthing things.
  • Watch the tired times. Late afternoon and before bed are when tempers and accidents peak for everyone.

A one-off, or a pattern?

One accident, or a shove on a rough day, is part of life with two small kids. It’s worth talking to your older one’s pediatrician if:

  • It keeps happening, or it’s getting rougher
  • The baby is actually getting hurt
  • Your older one is hurting other kids or adults too
  • You don’t feel okay leaving them in the same room for a moment

That isn’t a judgment on your child or on you. It’s exactly what pediatricians are there for, and help works better the earlier it starts. And if anything about the baby still worries you in the days after, call the baby’s doctor too.

Afterwards, for you

A lot of dads replay it: “I only looked away for a second.” Every parent looks away. Accidents happen in careful, loving homes all the time. If your partner was the one watching, the same is true for them. What matters is what you do next, and you’re already doing it.

When you tell your partner, stick to the plain facts: what happened, how the baby is now, and what you’ll change. Then do it together. Most kids who were rough with a baby brother or sister grow up close to them.

If moments like this keep leaving you shaky or on edge, that counts too. See going from one kid to two and worn down and running on empty.

If it gets dark

If you ever feel close to hurting one of your kids, put them somewhere safe and step away. Then call or text 988, or the Maternal Mental Health Hotline at 1-833-852-6262. Both are free, 24/7, and dads can call. Calling before something happens is always better than after. If anyone is in danger right now, call 911.

Common questions

What should I do if my toddler hurt the baby?

Check the baby first. Call 911 or go to the ER if the baby is hard to wake, passed out, has a seizure, throws up more than once, won't move an arm or leg, can't be settled, won't feed, or just doesn't seem like themselves. If you're not sure, call your pediatrician's after-hours line. Then calm down before you deal with your older one.

When should I call the doctor after my baby bumped their head?

If the baby is under two, call the doctor the same day even if they seem fine. Go to the ER right away for drowsiness or trouble waking, vomiting more than once, a seizure, passing out, unequal pupils, or a cry you can't settle. Keep a close eye on them for the next day, because some signs show up later.

How do I handle my older child after they hurt the baby?

Separate them and calm down first, then keep it short: the baby got hurt, we are gentle with the baby. Don't shame them or call them bad. If it was on purpose, name the feeling, like being mad that the baby gets so much of you. Later, practice gentle touch together, and give them a little time each day that's just theirs.

Is it normal for a toddler to hurt a new baby?

Rough moments are very common when a family goes from one child to two. Toddlers don't know their own strength, and jealousy comes out through their hands. Talk to your pediatrician if it keeps happening, gets rougher, or you don't feel okay leaving them in a room together.

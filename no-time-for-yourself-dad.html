Time for yourself

No time to yourself? Small ways to get some back

Work all day, kids the minute work ends, and maybe an hour on the couch before you fall asleep. For a lot of dads, that hour is the only time that's theirs. If that's your week, you're not lazy or selfish for wanting more. Everyone needs a little time to reset. Here's how to find some, even when there's no room.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Start here

  • Use the small pockets. The drive, a walk, the shower.
  • Ask for one set time a week. "I need an hour on Saturday to recharge. I'll cover Sunday morning for you."
  • Let go of the guilt. A rested dad is more patient and more present.

Why it matters

When you never get a minute, it builds up. Your fuse gets shorter, small things feel big, and you stop enjoying the moments you used to love. A little time to yourself isn't taking something away from your family. It's how you keep showing up for them.

Think small

An hour off may not be possible right now. Ten minutes usually is. Small pieces add up:

  • The car. Sit for five minutes in the driveway before you walk in, with music, a podcast, or nothing at all.
  • The walk. Take the long way to get the mail, or walk around the block on a lunch break.
  • The shower. Make it a real one. Door closed, phone out of reach.
  • Early or late. Some dads get up 20 minutes before the house wakes. Some keep 20 minutes after bedtime that's only theirs.
  • Doubling up. Headphones and a podcast while you do dishes or fold laundry.

Make your couch hour count

If the hour before bed is all you get, spend it on something that actually refills you. Endless scrolling often leaves you more tired, not less.

Try a show you love, a book, a game with a friend, a call with someone who makes you laugh, or simply sitting outside for a few minutes. And protect your sleep: staying up later to get more time back usually costs more than it gives.

Ask for a set time

  • Make it regular. One fixed block a week, like Saturday morning or one evening, is easier to keep than "when things calm down."
  • Trade. If you live with another adult, offer the same for them. Taking turns works best when both people get a break.
  • Use the help you have. A grandparent, a friend, a sitter, or a swap with another family for an hour or two.
  • Keep it simple. "I need an hour on Saturday to recharge. I'll cover Sunday morning for you" is enough.

Let go of the guilt

A lot of dads feel guilty taking any time while the house is busy. But a rested dad is more patient, more present, and more fun. Taking a break is part of doing the job well, not a break from it.

If you still can't catch your breath

Some seasons really do have no gaps, especially with a baby and a young child. If you're feeling numb, on edge, or low most days for more than two weeks, read worn down as a new dad and how to talk to your doctor.

More on finding a rhythm is in working from home with a newborn and going from one kid to two. Other dads get it too: PSI's free dads' groups meet online, and our message board is open any time.

Common questions

How do dads get time to themselves with a baby?

Think small. Five minutes in the car, a walk, a real shower, or 20 minutes before the house wakes all add up. A fixed weekly block, traded with another adult, is easier to keep than waiting for things to calm down.

Is it selfish to want time alone as a new dad?

No. Everyone needs time to reset. A rested dad is more patient and present, so a short break helps the whole family.

What if my only break is an hour before bed?

Spend it on something that refills you, like a show, a book, or a call with a friend, rather than endless scrolling. And protect your sleep.

What if I never get a break and feel worn down?

If you've felt numb, on edge, or low most days for more than two weeks, talk to a doctor. Peer support groups for dads can help too.

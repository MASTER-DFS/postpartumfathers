When a parent you're distant from reaches out

Maybe you and your mom or dad haven't talked in years. Maybe it's been strained for a long time. Then the baby arrives, and suddenly they want to be part of things. It can bring up hope, anger, sadness and worry all at once. Whatever you feel is okay, and whatever you decide can be the right call.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Start here

  • You don't have to answer today. "Thanks for reaching out. I need some time" is a full reply.
  • Decide with whoever you're raising the baby with. It affects your whole family.
  • Talk it through with someone outside it, like a friend, a counselor, or other dads on our message board.

Why a new baby stirs this up

Becoming a parent often brings your own childhood back into view. You might see your parent differently, for better or worse. You might want your child to know their grandparent, or want to protect them from what you went through. You can feel both.

Many dads feel torn. You want your kids to know their grandparent, but you're not ready to reopen everything from the past. That's a fair place to be, and you don't have to settle it all at once.

Take your time

A message or a call from an estranged parent can feel urgent. It isn't. You're allowed to wait until you've slept on it, talked it over, and know what you want. A short, kind holding reply is enough for now.

If you want to reconnect, go slow

  • Meet your parent first, without the baby. See where they're coming from. Do they want a relationship with you, or only with the baby? How they handle that first talk tells you a lot.
  • Start small. A few texts or a phone call before any visit.
  • Keep early visits short, and somewhere neutral, like a park or a coffee shop.
  • Say what you need up front. For example, call before coming over, or no comments about how you're raising the baby.
  • Keep the focus on now. A relationship with your kids doesn't have to mean digging up the past. You can keep old topics off the table until you're ready.
  • Notice how you feel after. Lighter, or drained? That tells you how fast to go.

It's okay to say not now, or no

Some relationships hurt more than they help. If the past involved abuse, addiction, or a parent who wasn't safe, your child's safety and your own peace come first. Keeping distance is a choice many parents make, and it says nothing bad about you as a son or a father.

"Not now" can change later. So can "yes." You can adjust as your family grows.

Protect your energy

You already have a baby, sleep loss, and maybe a job. Reconnecting takes energy too. It's fine to put this on the back burner until things feel steadier. For setting limits with family who are already around, see family visiting after the baby and saying no when you're stretched thin.

Get support

Old family hurt can be heavy to carry alone. A counselor can help you sort out what you want, and many have helped people through exactly this. Read finding a therapist as a dad. PSI's free dads' groups, held online, are another place to talk it through.

Common questions

What should I do when an estranged parent wants to see my baby?

You don't have to answer right away. Decide with whoever you're raising the baby with, and if you want to reconnect, meet your parent on your own first to understand why they're reaching out, then go slow.

Is it okay to keep my baby away from an estranged parent?

Yes. If the relationship was harmful or unsafe, your child's safety and your peace come first. Keeping distance is a choice many parents make, and you can revisit it later.

Can my kids know their grandparent without me reopening the past?

Yes. You can keep visits short and focused on the present, and keep old topics off the table until you are ready.

Why does having a baby bring up feelings about my own parents?

Becoming a parent often brings your own childhood back into view. You might want your child to know their grandparent, or want to protect them, and both can be true.

Where can I get help with family estrangement?

A counselor can help you sort out what you want. Peer support from other dads, like PSI's free online dads' groups, can help too.

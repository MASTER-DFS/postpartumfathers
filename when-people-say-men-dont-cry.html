Hard days and hard feelings

When you grew up hearing men don't cry

A lot of dads were raised to hold it all in and be the strong one. After a baby, that pressure gets heavier, and it can feel like there's no room to struggle. There is. Here's why holding it in gets so hard, and small ways to set some of it down.

If you're ever thinking about suicide or feel your family would be better off without you, please call or text 988 any time, day or night. If anyone is in danger right now, call 911. More crisis help.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Try this tonight

  • Name it to yourself. "This is a lot, and I'm tired." Saying it in your head counts.
  • Send one text to someone you trust: "Hey, this dad thing is harder than I thought. Got a minute this week?"
  • If it's heavy tonight, call or text 988. You don't have to be in danger to call.

Where the pressure comes from

A lot of us grew up hearing it, in one form or another. "Men don't cry." "Be strong for your family." "Just push through." "Don't complain, other people have it worse." Sometimes it came from our fathers, sometimes from friends, coaches, church or work. Often nobody said it at all. We just watched the men around us carry everything quietly, and learned to do the same.

Then the baby comes. Now there's even more to carry, less sleep, and a feeling that you have to be the steady one while everyone else falls apart.

What holding it in can turn into

Feelings you don't let out don't go away. In dads they often come out sideways:

  • A short fuse. Snapping at your partner, the kids or yourself over small things.
  • Going numb. Feeling flat, far away, or like you're just going through the motions.
  • Staying busy. Longer hours at work, always fixing something, never sitting still.
  • Drinking more, or spending more time on your phone, games or anything that helps you not feel.
  • Headaches, a sore back, a tight chest, or trouble sleeping with no clear reason.

None of this makes you weak or a bad dad. It's what happens when a person carries too much for too long without setting any of it down. See snapping at everyone since the baby and the signs in dads.

Strong and struggling can be true at the same time

Going to work exhausted, getting up for the night feeds, holding it together for your family: that is strength. Telling someone "I'm not doing great" takes strength too. It's not giving up. It's how you keep going for the long haul.

Think of the dads you respect most. If one of them told you he was struggling, you wouldn't think less of him. You'd want to help. Give yourself the same respect.

Ways to talk that don't feel like a big talk

You don't have to sit across from someone and pour your heart out. For a lot of men, it's easier to talk:

  • Side by side. In the car, on a walk, fixing something, or watching a game. Not having to look at each other helps.
  • With one person. A brother, a cousin, an old friend, a coworker who's also a dad. You don't need to tell everyone.
  • In a few words. "Man, this has been harder than I thought" is enough to open the door.
  • In writing. A text can be easier than saying it out loud. So can our message board for dads, where you don't use your real name.
  • With other dads who get it. Free support groups for dads are full of men who were sure they were the only one.

What to say

  • "I don't really know how to say this, but I haven't felt like myself since the baby."
  • "I'm holding it together for everyone, and I'm running on empty."
  • "I don't need you to fix it. I just needed to tell someone."

If you'd rather talk to a professional, your doctor is a good first step. You don't have to say the word depression. See how to talk to your doctor.

The example you set

Your kids will learn what a man does with hard feelings by watching you. Taking care of yourself, and asking for help when you need it, is one of the best things you can show them. It's a way of breaking a pattern, not just for you, but for them.

Common questions

Is it normal for dads to hide how they feel after a baby?

Yes. Many dads grew up learning to keep feelings to themselves and to be the strong one. After a baby, that pressure often gets heavier, and many dads struggle quietly.

Does asking for help make me weak?

No. Telling someone you're struggling takes strength, and getting support helps you keep going for your family. Most people respect a dad who takes care of himself.

How do I talk about it if I'm not a talker?

Keep it short and pick one person. Talking side by side, like in the car or on a walk, is often easier. A text or an anonymous message board also works.

How do I know if it's more than stress?

If you've felt angry, numb, hopeless or not like yourself for more than two weeks, or it's affecting work, sleep or home, talk to a doctor. Postpartum depression in dads is common and treatable.

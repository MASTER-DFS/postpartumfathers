Health conditions and disabilities

When your baby has a health condition or disability

Maybe you heard it before the birth. Maybe it came after, in a hallway, from a doctor using words you'd never heard. Either way, the plan in your head just changed. Dads often go straight into fixing mode and push their own feelings down. You're allowed to be scared, sad and still completely in love with your baby.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Start here

  • Start a notebook for appointments, questions and what each doctor said.
  • Write down one question for the next visit, and ask it even if it feels small.
  • Ask for a parent match. Parent to Parent USA pairs you with a parent who's been there.

It's OK to grieve the plan

Many parents feel a kind of grief after a diagnosis. Not for the baby, but for the future they'd pictured. That feeling can sit right next to fierce love. Both are normal, and having one doesn't cancel the other.

You may also feel guilt, anger, or a strange numbness. Let yourself feel it. Pushed-down feelings usually leak out later in other ways.

Become the organizer

There may be a lot of appointments, specialists and paperwork. Many dads find it steadying to take on part of this.

  • One notebook or binder for test results, medicines, doctors' names and phone numbers.
  • Questions written before each visit. Ask doctors to explain anything that doesn't make sense.
  • One shared calendar for appointments, so nothing falls through the cracks.

Share the load with your partner and talk about who handles what, so neither of you burns out.

Early intervention: help for young kids

If your child has a delay or disability, or you're worried about how they're growing, every state has an early intervention program for kids under 3. The CDC says you don't need a doctor's referral or a diagnosis to ask for a free evaluation. The CDC explains how to start.

For kids 3 and older, the local public school system can evaluate them for preschool services.

Find parents who've been there

Other parents of kids with the same condition can be the best support you'll find. Parent to Parent USA matches parents with a trained support parent through programs in many states. If your state doesn't have one, they suggest reaching out to a program in a neighboring state. Many conditions also have their own parent groups. Ask your baby's care team what they know of.

Your own tank matters

Caring for a child with medical needs is a marathon. Keep eating, sleeping when you can and staying in touch with friends. Work can get hard to juggle, so see work stress and a new baby.

  • Our message board, open any time.
  • PSI's free dads' groups, which meet online.
  • The Maternal Mental Health Hotline, 1-833-852-6262, free and 24/7, dads too.

Common questions

Is it normal to grieve after my baby's diagnosis?

Yes. Many parents grieve the future they had pictured, while loving their baby deeply. Both feelings can exist at once, and neither cancels the other.

How can a dad help when our baby has a health condition?

Many dads take on organizing: a binder for results and medicines, questions written before visits, and one shared calendar. Share the load with your partner so neither of you burns out.

Do I need a referral for early intervention?

No. The CDC says you don't need a doctor's referral or a diagnosis to request a free evaluation through your state's early intervention program for kids under 3.

Where can I talk to other parents of kids with special needs?

Parent to Parent USA matches parents with a trained support parent through state programs. Your baby's care team may also know of groups for your child's condition.

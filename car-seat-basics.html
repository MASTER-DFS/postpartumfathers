When the baby gets hurt

Car seat basics

Car seats are confusing, and the rules change as your kid grows. The most common surprise is finding out your kid outgrew their seat months ago. Here’s how to check, when to switch, and how to get the fit right, in plain terms.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Start here

  • Find the sticker on the side or back of the seat. It lists the height and weight limits.
  • Compare it to your kid today. Over either limit means a new seat.
  • Do the pinch test: no slack in the strap at the shoulder, chest clip at the armpits.

Has your kid outgrown the seat?

Kids grow fast, and a seat that fit in the spring may not fit by fall. Every seat has a sticker on the side or back with its height and weight limits, and the same limits are in the manual. If you can’t find the manual, search the brand and model number from the sticker online.

Check these every few months:

  • Weight and height. Weigh and measure your kid, or use the numbers from the last checkup, and compare them to the sticker. Going over either one means it’s time.
  • Rear-facing: many seats also say the top of the head must be at least an inch below the top of the seat shell. Your manual will say.
  • Forward-facing: the seat is outgrown when the tops of the ears reach the top of the seat, or the shoulders are above the top harness slots.
  • The date. Seats expire. The sticker shows the date it was made, and the manual or the maker says how many years it lasts.

If you just found out your kid outgrew their seat, you’re in good company. Most parents only learn this when someone points it out.

Which seat, when

  • Rear-facing for as long as possible, until your kid hits the top height or weight limit of the seat. Every baby under 1 rides rear-facing. Many kids can stay rear-facing until 2, 3 or older in a convertible seat. Bent legs are fine.
  • Forward-facing with a harness once they outgrow rear-facing. Always use the top tether strap if your car and seat have one.
  • A booster seat once they outgrow the harness, until the adult seat belt fits right. That’s usually around 4 feet 9 inches tall, somewhere between 8 and 12 years old.
  • The back seat for every kid through age 12.

Not sure which seat fits right now? NHTSA’s Car Seat Finder asks for your kid’s age, height and weight and tells you which type of seat they need. Their car seat page also has installation videos and recall lookups.

Getting the fit right

  • Harness height: rear-facing, the straps come out at or below the shoulders. Forward-facing, at or just above.
  • The pinch test: once buckled, try to pinch the strap at the shoulder. If you can pinch any slack, tighten it.
  • Chest clip at armpit level, not down on the belly.
  • No puffy coats or snowsuits under the straps. They squash flat in a crash and leave the harness loose. Buckle them in thin layers, then put the coat or a blanket on top.
  • The seat itself: installed with either the lower anchors or the seat belt, not both. Tug it at the belt path. It shouldn’t move more than an inch side to side.

Used seats and crashes

Avoid a used seat unless you know its whole history: never in a crash, not expired, no recalls, all its parts and the manual. A hand-me-down from someone you trust can be fine. A seat from a yard sale usually isn’t.

After a moderate or bad crash, replace the seat, even if it looks fine. Some makers say to replace it after any crash. Your manual will say, and many car insurance policies help pay for a new one.

Get it checked for free

Certified car seat technicians will check your seat and show you how to install it, usually free. Look up a station near you on NHTSA’s car seat page, or ask your local fire department or hospital. It takes about half an hour, and most parents learn at least one thing they were doing wrong.

If it gets dark

If you’re running on empty and things feel too heavy, call or text 988, or the Maternal Mental Health Hotline at 1-833-852-6262. Both are free, 24/7, and dads can call. If anyone is in danger right now, call 911.

Common questions

How do I know if my child has outgrown their car seat?

Check the sticker on the side or back of the seat for its height and weight limits, and compare them to your child. Rear-facing, many seats also need the top of the head at least an inch below the top of the shell. Forward-facing, the seat is outgrown when the tops of the ears reach the top of the seat or the shoulders are above the top harness slots.

How long should my child stay rear-facing?

As long as possible, until they reach the top height or weight limit of their rear-facing seat. Every baby under 1 rides rear-facing, and many kids can stay rear-facing until 2, 3 or older in a convertible seat. Bent legs are fine.

Do car seats expire?

Yes. The sticker on the seat shows the date it was made, and the manual or the maker tells you how many years it lasts. Plastic weakens over time, so don't use a seat past its date.

Where can I get my car seat checked for free?

Certified car seat technicians check seats for free in most places. Find a station on NHTSA's car seat page, or ask your local fire department or hospital.

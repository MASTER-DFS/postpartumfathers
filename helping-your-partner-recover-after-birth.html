Your partner

Helping your partner recover after the birth

Her body needs weeks to heal, and longer after a C-section. You can’t do the healing for her. But you can take almost everything else off her plate. Here’s how.

Heavy bleeding, chest pain, trouble breathing, a bad headache or thoughts of self-harm after birth need care right away. Call 911 if it can’t wait. More on getting help.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Try this tonight

  • Bring the baby to her for the feed, then take the baby back so she can sleep.
  • Fill her water bottle and put snacks and her charger within reach.
  • Write down when she takes pain medicine, so nobody has to remember at 3am.

What recovery really looks like

Her body just did something huge. Healing takes weeks, not days. After a C-section it is major surgery on top of a newborn. After a vaginal birth there can be stitches, soreness and bleeding for weeks.

She may look fine on the outside and still be hurting. She is also feeding the baby around the clock. Your job right now is simple: take everything else off her plate so she can heal.

Set up her spot

Pick one place where she spends most of the day, the bed or the couch. Keep it stocked so she doesn’t have to get up for every little thing.

  • A big water bottle, always full. Feeding makes her thirsty.
  • Snacks she can eat with one hand.
  • Phone charger, burp cloths, diapers and wipes within reach.
  • Pain medicine on a schedule. Write down the time she takes each dose, or set phone alarms, so nobody has to remember at 3am.

After a C-section

  • You do the lifting. Her doctor will usually tell her not to lift anything heavier than the baby for a while. That means the car seat, laundry baskets, groceries and older kids are yours.
  • Help her up. Getting out of bed hurts. Offer your arm, and a pillow to hold against her belly when she coughs or laughs.
  • Stairs and driving: ask her doctor what is OK and when. Every recovery is different.
  • Keep an eye on the cut. Redness, swelling, oozing or a fever are reasons to call the doctor.

What you can take over

  • Everything except feeding, if she’s nursing. Diapers, burping, changing, bathing, settling.
  • Bring the baby to her for night feeds, then take the baby back after so she can go straight back to sleep.
  • Meals, dishes, laundry and the trash. Simple is fine. Cereal counts.
  • The visitors. Keep visits short, and you be the one who says it’s time to go. See family visiting after the baby.
  • Her appointments. Put her checkups in your calendar and drive her if she can’t.

People offer to help. Say yes, and be specific. Copy one of these if it’s easier to send.

“Thank you for offering. A dinner we can heat up would help a lot this week. Could you drop it on the porch?”

“We'd love to see you. Could you keep it to about 30 minutes? She's still healing and tires out fast.”

Warning signs: get care right away

Most recoveries go fine. But some serious problems show up days or weeks after the birth. The CDC says to get medical care right away for any of these, up to a year after birth:

  • A headache that won’t go away or gets worse, or changes in vision
  • Dizziness or fainting
  • A fever of 100.4°F or higher
  • Trouble breathing, chest pain, or a fast-beating heart
  • Severe nausea and throwing up
  • Severe belly pain that doesn’t go away
  • Extreme swelling of the hands or face
  • Swelling, redness or pain in a leg or arm
  • Heavy bleeding (soaking through a pad in an hour, or clots the size of an egg or bigger) or bad-smelling discharge
  • Overwhelming tiredness
  • Thoughts of harming herself or the baby

Go with her, or call 911 if it can’t wait. Tell them she recently gave birth. That one sentence changes what they check for.

Her mood matters too

Crying and mood swings in the first two weeks are common. If sadness, worry or not feeling like herself lasts longer than that, or gets heavy, it may be postpartum depression or anxiety. It is common and it is treatable. Read helping your partner and when your partner is anxious all the time.

Don’t forget you

You can’t pour from an empty cup. Eat, drink water and grab sleep when you can. Splitting the nights into shifts helps both of you. If you’re running on fumes, read worn down and running on empty.

If it gets heavy

If either of you is struggling to cope, call or text the National Maternal Mental Health Hotline at 1-833-852-6262. It’s free, 24/7, and takes calls from partners too. If anyone is thinking about suicide, call or text 988. If anyone is in danger right now, call 911.

Common questions

How can I help my wife recover after giving birth?

Take everything off her plate except healing and feeding. Set up a spot with water, snacks and her phone charger, track her pain medicine times, handle diapers, meals and visitors, and bring the baby to her at night.

How do I help my partner after a C-section?

Do all the lifting, including the car seat and laundry. Help her in and out of bed, ask her doctor about stairs and driving, and call the doctor for redness, swelling or oozing at the cut, or a fever.

How long does it take to recover from birth?

Usually several weeks, and longer after a C-section or a hard birth. Every recovery is different, so follow what her doctor says.

What are the warning signs after birth?

The CDC says to get care right away for a headache that won't go away, vision changes, dizziness or fainting, fever of 100.4°F or higher, trouble breathing, chest pain, severe belly pain, leg or arm swelling, heavy bleeding, overwhelming tiredness, or thoughts of self-harm. Tell them she recently gave birth.

Supporting your partner

How to help your wife with postpartum depression

Watching the person you love struggle after a baby is one of the hardest things a dad goes through. Here's what helps, what to watch for, and how to keep yourself standing too.

Call 911 if she talks about harming herself or the baby, seems confused or out of touch with reality, sees or hears things that aren't there, or hasn't slept in days. More crisis help.

Baby blues or postpartum depression?

Many new mothers feel weepy and overwhelmed for the first couple of weeks. That's the baby blues, and it usually passes on its own. Postpartum depression is stronger and lasts longer. Signs include:

  • Crying or sadness most days
  • Anxiety, panic, or constant worry
  • Irritability, anger, or arguing more
  • Pulling away from you or the baby
  • Can't sleep even when the baby sleeps
  • Feeling worthless, guilty, or like a bad mother
  • Scary, unwanted thoughts she can't shake
  • Saying everyone would be better off without her

If signs last more than two weeks, or they're severe at any point, it's time to get her help.

If she says everyone would be better off without her, or talks about dying or not wanting to be here, take it seriously today. Call or text 988 together, or call the Maternal Mental Health Hotline at 1-833-852-6262. If she has a plan or you think she's in danger, call 911.

Many new moms with postpartum depression or anxiety get sudden, unwanted thoughts or images of something bad happening to the baby, and are horrified by them. These thoughts are common and treatable, and having them doesn't mean she'll act on them. Help her call her doctor or the hotline the same day. Call 911 if she seems to want to act on them or seems out of touch with reality.

What to say

  • "I've noticed you've been having a really hard time. It's not your fault."
  • "This happens to a lot of moms, and it gets better with help."
  • "You're a good mom. I'm on your side."
  • "Can I call the doctor with you, or for you?"

Try not to fix it, argue, or tell her to snap out of it. Just listening, without judging, is doing something.

What to do

  • Help her get care. Call her OB or midwife, or the National Maternal Mental Health Hotline (1-833-852-6262), which is free, 24/7, and takes calls and texts from partners too.
  • Protect her sleep. Take a night feed or give her a solid block of hours.
  • Take things off her plate without being asked: meals, laundry, visitors.
  • Bring in help. Let family and friends cover shifts.
  • Expect hard days. Crying and arguing are common. They don't mean your relationship is broken.

Look after yourself too

Dads whose partners have postpartum depression are at higher risk of depression themselves. Watch for the signs of postpartum depression in dads, and talk to someone. Free support groups for fathers are a good place to start.

Common questions

How do I know if my wife has postpartum depression?

Watch for sadness or crying most days, anxiety or panic, irritability, pulling away from you or the baby, trouble sleeping even when she can, feeling worthless or guilty, or saying she is a bad mother. If it lasts more than two weeks, it is more than the baby blues.

What should I say to my wife with postpartum depression?

Keep it simple and without blame: tell her what you have noticed, that it is not her fault, that it is common and treatable, and that you will help her get support. Then offer to make the first call with her.

When is postpartum depression an emergency?

Call 911 if she talks about harming herself or the baby, seems confused or out of touch with reality, sees or hears things that aren't there, or hasn't slept for days. These can be signs of postpartum psychosis, which is a medical emergency.

How can I take care of myself while supporting her?

Sleep in shifts, accept help from family and friends, keep talking to someone you trust, and watch for signs of depression in yourself. Partners of mothers with postpartum depression are at higher risk too.

Worried about him? How to help a new dad

This page is for partners, parents and friends. If a new dad in your life doesn't seem like himself, you're right to notice. About 1 in 10 dads go through postpartum depression, according to Postpartum Support International, and many never hear that it can happen to fathers. Here's what to look for and what to say.

If he talks about being better off gone or harming himself, call or text 988 now. You can call for him. If anyone is in danger right now, call 911. Warning signs.

Signs a new dad may be struggling

In men, depression and anxiety often don't look like sadness. You might notice:

  • Irritability, a short fuse, or anger
  • Pulling away from the family or friends
  • Working longer hours, or finding reasons to stay out
  • Drinking more, gaming, or endless scrolling
  • Seeming numb, flat, or hopeless
  • Trouble sleeping even when the baby sleeps
  • Constant worry, or headaches and stomach problems

You might also hear "I'm fine," "I'm just tired," or "she's the one who had the baby." A rough week is normal with a newborn. If several of these last more than two weeks, it's worth bringing up.

Why he might not say anything

Most of what new parents hear is about the mom and the baby, and that's how it should be. But it means a lot of dads never hear that fathers can struggle too. The check-ups and the forms usually go to mom. Many men also grew up hearing that they should just handle it. So he may not know what he's feeling has a name, or that help exists for him.

How to bring it up

  • Pick a calm moment, not the middle of a hard night. A drive or a walk can be easier than face to face.
  • Start with what you see, gently. "You've seemed really worn down lately. How are you doing?"
  • Listen more than you talk. He may not have words for it yet.
  • Let him know it's common and treatable, and that it doesn't make him a bad dad.
  • Offer one small next step, like sharing this site, or helping him book a doctor's appointment.

Try not to say: "snap out of it," "you have it easy compared to her," or "everyone's tired." He probably already thinks those things.

If you're his partner

  • Protect some sleep for him, the way you'd want him to protect yours. One full night can change how a conversation goes.
  • Ask about him, not just the baby. "How are you doing with all this?"
  • Make it a team problem. "We're both running on empty. Let's figure out how to get us both some help."
  • Look after yourself too. Both parents can struggle at the same time. If you're not feeling like yourself either, the hotline below is for you as well.

If you're his mom or dad

When your son becomes a dad, most of the calls and questions go to the baby and the new mom. That's natural, but it can leave him feeling invisible.

  • Ask how he's doing. "How are you holding up?" means a lot when everyone else only asks about the baby.
  • Offer something specific, like a meal, a load of laundry, or two hours with the baby so they can both rest.
  • Share, don't lecture. If you had a hard time when he was born, saying so can make it easier for him to talk.
  • Keep it between you. He'll open up more if he knows it won't go around the family.

If you're his friend or brother

  • Don't wait for him to reach out. New dads disappear from group chats. Keep inviting him, even if he says no.
  • Make it short and easy. A 20-minute coffee or a walk with the stroller is easier to say yes to than a night out.
  • Say it straight. "Hey, you've seemed off lately. You doing OK?" One honest question from a friend can open the door.

Words you can copy and send

  • "Thinking about you. How are you really doing with the baby?"
  • "No need to reply. Just want you to know I'm here, any time."
  • "I can take the baby for a couple of hours this weekend so you two can sleep. Which day works?"
  • "I read that a lot of new dads go through a rough patch. If that's you, I'm not going anywhere."

If he says he's fine

That's common, and it doesn't mean the talk failed. Leave the door open: "OK. If that changes, I'm here." Share one thing he can look at alone, like is this more than tired? Bring it up again in a week or two. If you want advice on what to do next, you can call the PSI HelpLine yourself at 1-800-944-4773. It's not a crisis line; a volunteer calls you back, 8am to 11pm Eastern.

If you're worried about his safety

Call or text 988 if he talks about being a burden, says the family would be better off without him, talks about harming himself, gives things away, or suddenly seems calm after a very dark stretch. You can call for someone you're worried about, or sit with him while he calls. Almost every call stays on the phone, and help is sent only if a life is in danger right then.

If there are guns in the home, read firearms and a new baby on storing them away for a while.

If anyone is in danger right now, call 911.

Look after yourself too

Caring for a newborn and worrying about someone you love is a lot. The Maternal Mental Health Hotline, 1-833-852-6262, is free, 24/7, and takes calls and texts from moms, dads and family.

Common questions

My husband seems depressed after the baby. What do I do?

Pick a calm moment, tell him what you've noticed, and listen. Let him know it's common in dads and treatable, and offer one small step, like booking a visit with his doctor. If he talks about harming himself, call or text 988.

My son just had a baby and isn't himself. How can I help?

Ask about him, not just the baby, and offer specific help like a meal or a few hours with the baby so they can rest. Gently mention that many new dads struggle and that help exists.

Can fathers get postpartum depression?

Yes. About 1 in 10 dads experience postpartum depression, according to Postpartum Support International. It's treatable.

What if he says he's fine?

Leave the door open, share one thing he can read on his own, and check in again in a week or two. You can call the PSI HelpLine at 1-800-944-4773 for advice.

What if I'm worried about a new dad's safety?

If he talks about being better off gone or harming himself, call or text 988. You can call for someone you're worried about. If anyone is in danger right now, call 911.

Feeling boxed in

Feel like family life is holding you back?

You love your kids. But some days it feels like the rest of you got put on hold. Your goals, your friends, your fun. And on the rare night you do get out, the phone keeps going: when will you be home, why is it taking so long, why didn’t you pick up? Many dads feel this. It doesn’t make you a bad dad, and it can get better.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Start here

  • Agree on a time before you go out, and add a little extra.
  • Send one text before you start driving: “Leaving now, home around 9:40.” Then put the phone away.
  • Pick one small goal for this month that’s just yours.

Why it feels this way

  • Your time isn’t yours. Work, then kids, then sleep. Plans for yourself keep sliding to next week.
  • Your goals are on hold. A class, a better job, getting fit, a hobby. It can feel like everyone else is moving and you’re standing still.
  • Time out comes with strings. When every outing comes with check-in calls, it stops feeling like a break.

It’s okay to feel this. Wanting a life of your own isn’t selfish. It’s part of staying well enough to show up for your family.

Why the calls keep coming

When one parent is home alone with small kids, the hours can feel long. A late return can stir up worry, or plain tiredness. They may be counting the minutes until there’s another adult in the house.

So “When will you be home?” often means “I’m worn out” or “I was worried,” not “you can’t go.” You’re both stretched. Seeing it that way doesn’t make the calls less annoying, but it makes them easier to talk about.

Make going out easier on both of you

  • Plan it ahead. Agree on it a day or two before, not as you walk out the door.
  • Give a time, and pad it. If you think 9, say 9:30. Coming home early feels good for everyone.
  • Text once before you drive. “Leaving now, home around 9:40.” Then put the phone away.
  • Never pick up the phone while driving. Many phones have a driving mode that sends an automatic reply, like “Driving, I’ll call when I park.” A late answer is better than a crash.
  • Trade turns. If you get a night out, your partner gets one too. Same rules both ways.

Talk about it without a fight

Pick a calm moment, not when you’re leaving or just walking in. Start with “we”: “We both need some time away. How can we make that work?”

Ask what makes it hard for them when you’re gone. Say what you need, like a few hours where you can switch off. Then agree on a check-in plan you can both live with. If talks keep turning into arguments, see shutting down or snapping at home.

Your goals are paused, not gone

  • Shrink it. Twenty minutes a day on the thing you care about, early morning or after the kids are down.
  • Write it down. One goal for the next three months. Just one.
  • Put it on the shared calendar, so it’s a plan you both know about, not a surprise.
  • Remember this stage is short. Kids sleep better, need less, and time opens up as they grow.

For ways to find small pockets of time, read no time to yourself. If you’re missing your old life, read when fatherhood isn’t what you expected. If you barely leave the house at all, see when home starts to feel like a prison.

When it’s more than frustration

Feeling boxed in now and then is normal. It may be more than a rough patch if you feel trapped all the time, snap at everyone, dread going home, or keep thinking about escaping or disappearing.

  • Talk to your doctor. Here’s how to bring it up.
  • A counselor, on your own or as a couple. See finding a therapist.
  • The Maternal Mental Health Hotline, 1-833-852-6262, free and 24/7, dads too.
  • 988, call or text, if you’re having thoughts of not wanting to be here.

Read more about the signs in dads and snapping at everyone. This stage is hard, and it does change.

Common questions

Is it normal to feel like my family is holding me back?

Yes. Many dads feel their goals, friends and fun got put on hold after kids. It doesn't mean you don't love your family. Small, regular pockets of time for each parent help, and time opens up as the kids grow.

Why does my partner keep calling when I'm out?

When one parent is home alone with small kids, the hours feel long. The calls often come from worry or exhaustion, not from wanting to control you. Agreeing on a time and a quick text before you head home usually helps.

Should I answer the phone while driving?

No. Send one text before you start driving, then put the phone away. Many phones have a driving mode that sends an automatic reply. A late answer is better than a crash.

How do I go out without it turning into a fight?

Plan it a day or two ahead, give a time and pad it, text before you drive, and trade turns so your partner gets time away too. Talk about it at a calm moment, not on your way out.

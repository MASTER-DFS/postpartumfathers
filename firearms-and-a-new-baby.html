Firearms and a new baby in the house

This page is written on the assumption that you own firearms, that you intend to keep owning them, and that nobody needs to talk you into or out of anything. It is about storage, and about one option a lot of owners do not know exists. Every group linked here is run by gun owners.

Why a baby changes the question

Not because a newborn can reach anything. A newborn cannot roll over. The reason is that the habits you set in the next few months are the ones still running when that kid is three and has worked out how to climb a dresser, and nobody remembers to change the system on the exact day it starts to matter.

There is a second thing that is less often said. Running on four hours of sleep, people put things down in places they would not normally put them. The pistol that always goes in the safe ends up on top of it because the baby started screaming halfway through.

That is not carelessness, it is what tiredness does to everybody, and it is the reason a system that works when you are exhausted beats a system that works when you are sharp.

Storage that holds up

  • Every gun, every night, not just most of them. The one that gets missed is almost always the carry gun at the end of a long day.
  • Locked, and locked with something a kid cannot defeat. A cable lock through the action, a trigger lock, a lock box or a safe. Project ChildSafe has handed out more than 42 million free locks, and most police departments will give you one for nothing if you walk in and ask.
  • Ammunition stored separately where you reasonably can. It adds a step, and steps are the whole idea.
  • The nightstand is the one to rethink, and the answer is usually not giving it up. A quick-access safe with a fingerprint or keypad lock keeps it to hand and makes it a non-event when a toddler goes through that drawer.
  • Tell your partner where things are and how they work. Whoever is up at 3am alone with the baby should not be the person who does not know.

When your kid starts asking

It usually starts around three or four, with a question out of nowhere or a finger pointed at something on television. The instinct is to shut it down. Try not to. A gun that is never discussed becomes the most interesting object in the house, and secrecy is what makes a kid go looking.

Plain answers work. They are real, they are locked up, they are not toys, and if you ever want to see one you ask me and we will do it together. Then actually do that when they ask, because a kid whose curiosity gets answered has no reason to go hunting for it.

Teach them the rule as well. The one most commonly taught, by sheriffs’ departments and schools across the country, is four steps: stop, don’t touch, leave the area, tell an adult. Practice it rather than saying it once. Ask it at random, in the car, the way you would practice what to do in a fire.

And the honest part. Plenty of children who can recite that rule perfectly will still reach for a gun when there is no adult in the room. That is not a failure of teaching, it is what being five is. So the teaching is the second layer and the lock is the first, and they are not interchangeable.

One more, for a bit later on. Once your kid starts going to other houses, ask the parents how guns are stored there. It is a normal question for a gun owner to ask, and coming from you it lands better than it does from anyone else.

The option a lot of owners do not know about

Gun owners have quietly done this for each other for years. A buddy going through a divorce, a bad stretch after a death in the family, somebody who just says I would rather not have these in the house for a while. The guns go to someone else for a few weeks and then they come back.

What is newer is that there is now a formal way to do it. Hold My Guns connects owners with licensed dealers and ranges that store firearms temporarily. You keep ownership, you sign a storage agreement, and nobody asks you why. Their partner map shows where it is available.

One practical warning before you hand anything to a friend instead. In some states, passing a firearm to another person counts as a transfer with legal requirements attached, even between people who have known each other thirty years. Rules vary a lot. Check your own state first, and that legal wrinkle is part of why the dealer-based programs exist at all.

Nothing about this is permanent and nothing about it is a judgment. It is the same instinct as handing someone your keys at the end of a long night.

If you are the one having a hard time

The months after a baby arrives flatten a lot of fathers, and in men it usually shows up as a short fuse and feeling numb rather than as crying. If that is where you are, the main thing worth knowing is that putting some distance between yourself and a bad hour is a decision people make while they are still thinking clearly, not one they make at the bottom.

Walk the Talk America is worth a look. They work specifically with gun owners, they do not lobby, and they run a free anonymous mental health check plus a directory of providers who actually understand firearm ownership. That last part matters more than it sounds, because plenty of men avoid talking to anyone at all rather than sit through a conversation where owning guns becomes the topic.

If things are worse than that right now, call or text 988. You do not have to be in crisis to use it, and they will not send anyone to your door for calling.

There is more on what this looks like in the signs in dads and worn down as a new dad, and on finding someone to talk to in finding a therapist.

Where this site stands

Nowhere, on the politics. This page exists because dads asked, and because storage is one of the few things on this site that protects a child and a father at the same time. Every organization linked above is run by and for gun owners. We are not going to tell you what to own.

Common questions

How should I store guns with a new baby in the house?

Locked, with a device the child cannot defeat, and stored so that getting to a gun takes a deliberate act. A newborn cannot reach anything, but the habits you build now are the ones running when that child is three and climbing. Free locks are available through Project ChildSafe, and most law enforcement agencies hand them out.

Is a nightstand gun a problem once there are kids?

It is the spot most people have to rethink, because the whole point of it is speed. The usual answer is not giving it up, it is a quick-access safe with a fingerprint or keypad lock, which keeps the gun to hand while making it a non-event if a toddler opens that drawer.

Should I store my guns somewhere else for a while?

It is worth knowing the option exists. Gun owners have done this for each other for years, through a divorce, a bad stretch, a death in the family. Hold My Guns connects owners with licensed dealers and ranges that hold firearms temporarily. The guns stay yours and nobody asks why.

Can I just leave my guns with a friend for a few weeks?

Check your own state first. In some states handing a firearm to another person, even a friend, counts as a transfer with legal requirements attached, which is part of why the dealer-based storage programs exist. Worth a call before you load the truck.

Is teaching my kids about guns enough?

Teaching matters and should start early, but it works alongside locking, not instead of it. Children are curious, their friends are curious, and a child who knows the rule perfectly at the kitchen table can still be a child in a bedroom with nobody watching.

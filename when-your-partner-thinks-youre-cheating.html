Your partner

When your partner thinks you’re cheating because you travel

You’re away for work, and she’s home with the baby. Then come the questions about who you’re with, or she says she thinks there’s someone else. If there isn’t, that can hurt. It’s also more common after a baby than most people think, and with patience on both sides, trust can come back.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Try this tonight

  • Don’t argue it out over text. Call, or say you want to talk properly when you can.
  • Answer once, calmly: “There’s no one else. I’m here, for you and the baby.”
  • Call when you said you’d call. Small, steady things rebuild trust.

Why trust can get shaky after a baby

After a baby, one of you is often home, worn out, and cut off from the outside world. The other is out at work, in airports and hotels, talking to adults all day. From home, that life can look a lot more free than it feels. Add short nights and a body still healing, and it’s easy for a worried mind to fill in the blanks.

Innocent things can look different from far away, too. A friendly coworker who brings in food or sends a small gift home for the kids is just being kind, but to someone tired and home alone with a baby it can look like more, so it helps to mention people like that openly, before they turn into a question.

Anxiety after a baby can also show up as worry that won’t switch off. For some people that worry lands on the relationship. So the question “Is there someone else?” is often fear talking, not a verdict on you. That doesn’t make it easy to hear. But it can help you answer the fear instead of fighting the accusation.

It’s okay that it hurts

Being doubted when you’ve done nothing wrong can sting. You may feel angry, hurt, or tired of explaining. That’s normal. The urge is to defend yourself and win the argument. What usually helps more is calm reassurance and steady actions over time. Trust comes back through what you do, not through proving a point.

How to answer it

  • Stay calm and say it once. A clear, kind answer lands better than a long defense.
  • Listen for what’s underneath. Often it’s “I feel alone when you’re gone” or “I’m scared of losing you.” Answer that.
  • Talk by voice or video, not text. Text strips out your tone, and things go sideways fast.
  • Never in front of the kids. If it comes up with them around, say you want to talk later, and then do.
  • Don’t mock the worry or brush it off. Even if it isn’t true, the feeling behind it is real.

“There's no one else. I'm sorry you've been carrying that worry while I'm away. I'm here, for you and for the baby.”

“It sounds like being home alone while I travel is really hard. Can we talk about what would help when I'm gone?”

Make being away feel less far

  • Share your plans ahead of time. Where you’re staying, when you’re working late, when you’re free to talk.
  • Call when you said you would. A short call at the same time each evening means more than a long one that may or may not happen.
  • Be part of bedtime. A quick video call to say goodnight keeps you in the routine.
  • Send small check-ins. A photo, a “thinking of you,” a question about the baby’s day.
  • Ask what would help. Some couples like knowing more about the day. Agree on it together, so it feels like closeness, not checking up.

For more on planning a trip and keeping home running, see traveling for work with a new baby.

Rebuilding trust, together

Trust is a two-way street. On your side: keep small promises, include her in your plans, and make real time together when you’re home, even if it’s just a walk or a meal after the kids are down.

It also helps to say how the doubts land for you, calmly and at a good time. Something like “I want you to feel safe with me. When I’m questioned a lot, I start to feel like nothing I do is enough. Can we work on this together?” That isn’t blame. It’s letting her in on your side.

“I want you to feel safe with me. What would help you feel more secure while I'm traveling?”

If talks keep turning into fights, see shutting down or snapping at home for ways to reset.

When it’s a sign one of you needs more help

If the worry is new since the baby, hard to calm, or showing up in lots of other ways, like checking on the baby over and over or not being able to rest, it may be postpartum anxiety. That’s common and very treatable. See when your partner is anxious all the time for how to help.

If the same argument keeps coming back, a couples counselor can help you both feel heard. Going isn’t a sign you’re failing. See finding a therapist, and paying for it. And if all of this is wearing you down, that counts too. See worn down and running on empty. PSI’s free dads’ groups meet online, and our message board is open any time.

Many couples go through a stretch like this after a baby, and come out of it with more trust than before.

If it gets dark

If you’re thinking about suicide, or that your family would be better off without you, call or text 988. It’s free, 24/7, and you don’t have to be in crisis to call.

If either of you ever feels unsafe at home, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is free and confidential, 24/7: call 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788. If anyone is in danger right now, call 911.

Common questions

Why does my wife think I'm cheating when I travel for work?

After a baby, the partner at home is often exhausted and cut off, while the other is out in the world. Short nights and anxiety make it easy for a worried mind to fill in the blanks. It is often fear of losing you, not a verdict on you.

How do I reassure my partner I'm not cheating?

Stay calm and say it clearly, once. Listen for the fear underneath and answer that. Talk by voice or video, not text, and never in front of the kids. Then back it up with steady actions: share your plans, call when you said you would, and make real time together at home.

Can postpartum anxiety make someone jealous or suspicious?

It can. Anxiety after a baby often shows up as worry that won't switch off, and for some people that worry lands on the relationship. If it's new since the baby and hard to calm, it's worth talking to a doctor. Postpartum anxiety is common and very treatable.

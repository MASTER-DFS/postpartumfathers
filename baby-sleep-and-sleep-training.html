The baby

Baby sleep and sleep training

Nobody’s sleeping, and everyone has an opinion. Should you sleep train? When? Which way? And what if you and your partner don’t agree? Here’s a plain guide, with no side taken.

If the crying is pushing you to the edge, put the baby down safely in the crib and step away for a few minutes. Never shake a baby.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Try this tonight

  • Don’t decide sleep plans at 3am. Talk about it tomorrow, in daylight.
  • Split tonight into shifts so each of you gets one real stretch of sleep.
  • Start a short bedtime routine: bath or wipe-down, feed, song, bed.

First, what’s normal

Newborns wake a lot. They need to eat every few hours, day and night. That’s not a sleep problem, it’s how babies work. Most babies aren’t ready for any kind of sleep training until somewhere around 4 to 6 months. Ask your baby’s doctor before you start.

Whatever you do, safe sleep still comes first: on their back, in their own crib, nothing else in it. See safe sleep and the fear of SIDS.

The main approaches

There’s no single right way. Here are the common ones, from most hands-on to least.

  • No training. You keep responding to every wake-up and let sleep come with time. Many families do this, and it’s a fine choice.
  • Bedtime routine and fading. A short, same-every-night routine. A parent stays in the room and slowly does less, or sits a little farther away each night.
  • Check-ins (graduated). Put the baby down awake, leave, and go back for short check-ins at longer and longer gaps.
  • Cry it out. Put the baby down awake after the routine and don’t go back in until morning or the next feed.

Studies of the gentler and check-in methods have found no long-term harm to babies or to the bond with their parents. And better sleep tends to help parents’ mood. But no method is required. A plan both of you can stick with matters more than which one you pick.

When you two don’t agree

This is one of the most common things new parents disagree about. One parent can’t stand hearing the baby cry. The other is desperate for sleep. Both of those come from caring about the baby, and about the family.

  • Talk about it in the daytime, not at 3am when you’re both wrecked.
  • Start with what you agree on. “We both want the baby to be OK, and we both need more sleep.”
  • Ask what worries them about the other approach, and really listen.
  • Look for the middle. A gentle method, or checking in, is often something both parents can live with.
  • Ask the baby’s doctor together. A neutral voice can take the heat out of it.
  • Try it for one or two weeks, then check in on how it’s going. Nothing has to be forever.

“I know we see this differently. Can we pick one thing to try for two weeks and then talk about how it's going?”

If you can’t agree, it’s OK to hold off. Splitting the nights so each of you gets one real stretch of sleep helps either way. See taking night shifts with a newborn.

Things that help any baby sleep

  • Same short routine every night. Bath, feed, book, song, bed.
  • Bright days, dark nights. Daylight and noise by day, dim and quiet at night.
  • Keep night feeds boring. Low light, little talking.
  • Put the baby down drowsy, when you can, not always fully asleep.

When the crying gets to you

If a crying baby is pushing you to the edge, it’s OK to put them down safely in the crib and step away for a few minutes. Never shake a baby. See when the crying won’t stop. If you’re so short on sleep you feel hopeless or numb, read is this more than tired?

If it gets heavy

If either of you is struggling to cope, call or text the National Maternal Mental Health Hotline at 1-833-852-6262, free and 24/7, dads too. If anyone is thinking about suicide, call or text 988. If anyone is in danger right now, call 911.

Common questions

When can you start sleep training a baby?

Most babies aren't ready until somewhere around 4 to 6 months. Ask your baby's doctor before you start, and keep following safe sleep.

Is cry it out harmful?

Studies of the gentler and check-in methods have found no long-term harm to babies or the parent bond. But no method is required, and many families choose not to sleep train at all.

What if my partner and I disagree about sleep training?

It's one of the most common things parents disagree about. Talk in the daytime, start with what you agree on, look for a middle option like a gentle method, ask the baby's doctor together, and try it for one or two weeks before checking in.

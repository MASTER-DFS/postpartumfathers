Your partner

When your partner talks about leaving

Hearing your partner say she wants to leave, or isn’t sure she wants to stay, is hard at any time. With a new baby at home it can feel like the floor just dropped. Take a breath. You don’t have to fix this tonight, and it doesn’t always mean it’s over.

If you feel you can’t go on, please call or text 988 any time, day or night. If anyone is in danger right now, call 911. More on getting through the dark moments.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Try this tonight

  • Don't try to settle it tonight. Nothing gets decided well at 3am on no sleep.
  • Say one calm thing. “I hear you. I don’t want to lose us. Can we talk when we’ve both slept?”
  • Then do one real thing for her tomorrow, like the morning shift, without being asked.

Why it can come up after a baby

The first year with a baby is one of the hardest stretches most couples ever go through. When someone says “I don’t know if I can do this anymore,” it often comes from the bottom of that exhaustion. It doesn’t always mean the relationship is over.

  • Nobody is sleeping. Everything feels bigger, more hopeless and more permanent at 3am.
  • One or both of you may feel alone with it. When you’re both running on empty, it’s easy for each person to feel they’re carrying more.
  • There’s no time left for the two of you. Every conversation is about feeds, diapers and who’s more tired.
  • Postpartum depression or anxiety can color everything. In either parent, it can make the whole relationship feel hopeless, even when the love is still there. See helping your partner and the signs in dads.

None of this makes it hurt less. But it does mean there’s often more room to turn things around than it feels like tonight.

What helps in the first few days

  • Listen before you defend yourself. Ask “What’s been the hardest part for you?” and let the answer land, even if parts of it sting.
  • Say what you want, simply. “I don’t want to lose us. I want to work on this.” Once is enough. You don’t have to win the argument.
  • Take one real thing off her plate. Not a promise, an action. Take a night feed, the dishes, or the morning shift, and keep doing it.
  • Fix the sleep first if you can. Hard talks go better after rest. Try splitting the nights into shifts so each of you gets one solid block.
  • Suggest seeing someone together. “Would you be willing to talk to a counselor with me?” Many couples counselors see new parents, and many do video sessions from home.
  • Hold off on big decisions. Moving out, splitting money or telling everyone can usually wait until you’ve both slept and talked it through properly.

What usually makes it worse

  • Begging, pleading or making promises you can’t keep.
  • Threats, ultimatums, or raising your voice.
  • Checking her phone or tracking where she goes.
  • Getting family or friends to pressure her.
  • Numbing it with drinking or staying out late. See drinking more since the baby.

If you feel yourself sliding into any of these, step away, take a breath, and come back to it when you’re calmer. Shutting down or snapping at home has ways to hit pause.

Words that can open the door

If it’s hard to say out loud, send it. Copy one and change anything that doesn’t sound like you.

“I heard what you said, and I'm not going to argue about it tonight. I don't want to lose us. Can we talk when we've both had some sleep?”

“I know I haven't seen how much you've been carrying. I want to do better. What's one thing I can take off your plate this week?”

“Would you be willing to talk to a counselor with me? I'll find someone and set it up.”

Finding a counselor for the two of you

  • Ask your doctor or hers for a referral to a counselor who works with couples and new parents.
  • Check your job’s Employee Assistance Program. Many include a few free, confidential sessions, and some cover couples counseling.
  • Search the PSI directory for counselors trained in the months around a birth.
  • Money tight? See getting help without insurance and finding a therapist, and paying for it.

If your partner isn’t ready to go, you can still go on your own. Talking it through with someone helps you stay steady, whatever happens next.

Look after yourself through this

Hearing this with a newborn at home can knock the wind out of you. Panic, grief, anger and not being able to eat or sleep are all common. You need support too, and not just from her.

  • Tell one person you trust that things are hard at home. You don’t have to share every detail.
  • Talk to your own doctor if you’re not sleeping or eating, or can’t function at work. See how to talk to your doctor.
  • Talk to other dads. PSI’s free dads’ groups meet online, and our message board is open any time.
  • Keep showing up for your baby. Feeds, baths, walks. Whatever happens between the two of you, that bond is yours.

If you do end up separating

Sometimes, even after real effort, couples decide to live apart. That’s hard, and it doesn’t make you a failure as a dad. Your baby still needs you, and you can still be a big part of their life.

  • Get advice before you move out or sign anything. LawHelp.org lists free legal aid in every state, or call 211 and ask for family legal help.
  • Keep things calm in front of the baby, and keep your time with them steady.
  • Read not living with your baby for ways to stay close and co-parent calmly.

If it gets dark

Call or text 988 if you feel you can’t go on without her, that your family would be better off without you, or you’re thinking about harming yourself. It’s free, 24/7, and you don’t have to be in crisis to call. A breakup or the threat of one is one of the hardest things a person can face, and reaching out is the strong move.

If there are guns in the home, read firearms and a new baby on storing them away for a while.

If either of you ever feels unsafe at home, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is free and confidential, 24/7: call 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788. If anyone is in danger right now, call 911.

Common questions

Why does my wife want to leave after having a baby?

The first year with a baby is one of the hardest times for most couples. Exhaustion, feeling alone with the load, no time together, and postpartum depression or anxiety in either parent can all make the relationship feel hopeless. It doesn't always mean it's over.

What should I say if my partner says she wants to leave?

Listen first and ask what's been hardest for her. Say simply that you don't want to lose the relationship and want to work on it, take one real task off her plate, and suggest talking to a counselor together.

Should we make big decisions about the relationship with a newborn?

Where you can, hold off on big decisions like moving out until you've both had some sleep and a chance to talk it through, ideally with a counselor.

Where can we find couples counseling after a baby?

Ask your doctor for a referral, check your job's Employee Assistance Program, or search the PSI directory for counselors who work with new parents. Many offer video sessions.

What if I feel like I can't go on?

Call or text 988 any time. It's free and confidential. If anyone is in danger right now, call 911.

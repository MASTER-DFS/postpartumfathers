Pregnancy and infant loss

Grieving a pregnancy loss as a dad

Losing a pregnancy or a baby is one of the hardest things a family can go through. People often ask how mom is doing and forget to ask the dad. Your grief is real too, whether the loss came early or late, and you don't have to carry it on your own.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

You are not alone in this

Early pregnancy loss is more common than most people realize. It happens in about 1 in 10 confirmed pregnancies, according to the National Institutes of Health. Most early losses come from a random chromosome problem at conception. Things like working, exercising, or sex do not cause miscarriage. It is not your fault, and it is not your partner's fault.

What grief can look like for dads

Many fathers feel they have to be the strong one. They handle the calls, the appointments and the house, and put their own feelings away for later. Grief often shows up anyway, sometimes weeks or months on. It can look like:

  • Feeling numb, or not feeling anything at all
  • A short fuse, or anger that comes out of nowhere
  • Throwing yourself into work, or wanting to be anywhere but home
  • Trouble sleeping, or not wanting to get out of bed
  • Guilt, or going over what you could have done differently
  • A hard time around due dates, other people's baby news, or the hospital

All of these are normal parts of grief. There is no right way or right timeline.

Things that can help

  • Say it out loud. To your partner, a friend, a family member, or a counselor. "I'm sad too" is enough.
  • Grieve side by side. You and your partner may grieve in different ways and at different times. That's okay. Keep talking, even in short check-ins.
  • Take time off if you can. Ask your work about bereavement leave or using sick time. You're allowed to need a few days.
  • Mark the loss your way. Some families name the baby, plant something, keep a small keepsake, or light a candle on the due date.
  • Look after the basics. Eat, sleep when you can, get outside, and go easy on the drinking.

Free support groups

Postpartum Support International runs free online groups led by trained facilitators, including a pregnancy and infant loss group for parents. They’re held on video, cameras on for introductions, and each session is capped, so join on time. The groups are free: if the sign-up page offers a paid plan, skip it. If your partner is pregnant again after a loss, PSI also runs a pregnancy after loss group, which is for the person who is pregnant. For dads whose baby died, Sad Dads Club is a nonprofit started by fathers who lost daughters to stillbirth. It offers free peer support online and in person, and helps dads reach counseling at no cost to them. Other dads also share on our community board, any time of day.

When grief gets heavier

Grief comes in waves, and that's expected. But if you're feeling hopeless, can't function day to day, or the low feeling isn't easing after a few weeks, talk to a doctor or counselor. Read how to talk to your doctor and finding a therapist as a dad. If you're having thoughts of not wanting to be here, call or text 988 now.

Common questions

Do dads grieve after a miscarriage?

Yes. Fathers grieve pregnancy loss too, though many hide it to support their partner. Grief can show up as numbness, anger, or throwing yourself into work.

Is a miscarriage anyone's fault?

No. Most early losses come from a random chromosome problem at conception. Working, exercising, or sex do not cause miscarriage.

Where can dads find support after pregnancy loss?

Postpartum Support International runs free online support groups for parents after pregnancy and infant loss. You can also talk to your doctor, a counselor, or call or text 988.

How long does grief after a pregnancy loss last?

There is no set timeline. Grief comes in waves and can return around due dates. If you feel hopeless or can't function after a few weeks, talk to a doctor or counselor.

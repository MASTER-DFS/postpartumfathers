Supporting your partner

Postpartum psychosis: what partners need to know

Postpartum psychosis is rare. Postpartum Support International says it happens in about 1 to 2 of every 1,000 births. But it comes on fast, it's a medical emergency, and partners are often the first to notice. The good news: with quick treatment, most women fully recover.

This is a medical emergency. If you see these signs, call 911 or go to the emergency room. Say she recently gave birth. Stay with her, and keep the baby safe with you or another adult. Not sure? Call or text 988 and describe what you’re seeing.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Signs to watch for

  • Seeing or hearing things that aren't there
  • Strange beliefs that aren't true, like the baby being in danger from someone, or being someone else's
  • Seeming confused, or not knowing where she is or what time it is
  • Talking fast, racing from one idea to the next
  • Very high energy or not needing sleep, even for days
  • Fast mood swings, agitation, or acting very suspicious or fearful
  • Just not seeming like herself in a way that scares you

What it is

Postpartum psychosis is a serious but treatable illness that usually starts in the first two weeks after birth, though it can start any time in the first year. It's different from postpartum depression. A mom with psychosis can lose touch with what's real, and she may not be able to tell that anything is wrong.

That's why a partner matters so much here. You may be the one who sees it first.

Some moms are at higher risk, especially those with bipolar disorder, a past episode of psychosis, or a family history of either. Long stretches without sleep can add to the risk. But it can happen to anyone, even with no history at all.

What to do

  • Get emergency help now. Call 911 or take her to the nearest emergency room. Tell them she recently had a baby.
  • Don't leave her alone, and don't leave her alone with the baby until she's seen.
  • Stay calm and kind. Don't argue with what she believes. Say, "I'm here. We're going to get you some help."
  • Make the home safer. If there are guns in the house, get them out for now. Here's how other gun owners do it. Lock up medicines too.
  • Not sure if it's an emergency? Call or text 988, or the Maternal Mental Health Hotline at 1-833-852-6262, and describe what you're seeing.

After she gets care

Treatment often starts in the hospital and usually includes medication. Recovery takes time, but most women get completely well. Many go on to have more children, with a plan made ahead of time with their doctors.

While she recovers, you may be running the home, caring for the baby and handling the paperwork. Accept every offer of help. Ask the care team what to watch for when she comes home, and who to call.

Some families also find it helps to talk to others who've been through it. Postpartum Support International has free online support groups for moms recovering from psychosis, and one for families touched by it.

Look after yourself too

Seeing someone you love like this can be frightening, and it can stay with you afterward. That's a normal reaction, not weakness. Talk to someone. PSI's free dads' groups meet online, the Maternal Mental Health Hotline at 1-833-852-6262 takes calls from partners 24/7, and our message board is open any time. If it's weighing on you, read after a scary birth too.

Common questions

What are the signs of postpartum psychosis?

Seeing or hearing things that aren't there, strange beliefs that aren't true, confusion, racing speech, very high energy or not needing sleep, extreme mood swings, and seeming not like herself. It usually starts within the first two weeks after birth, but can start any time in the first year.

How common is postpartum psychosis?

It is rare. Postpartum Support International says it happens in about 1 to 2 of every 1,000 births. It is a medical emergency, and it is treatable.

What should I do if I think my wife has postpartum psychosis?

Call 911 or take her to the emergency room and say she recently gave birth. Stay with her and keep the baby safe with you or another adult. If you're not sure, call or text 988 and describe what you're seeing.

Do women recover from postpartum psychosis?

Yes. With quick treatment, most women fully recover. Many go on to have more children, with a plan made ahead of time with their doctors.

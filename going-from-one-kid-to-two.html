Baby number two

Going from one kid to two? You’ve got this

Everyone said the second one is easier. Then you're bouncing a newborn while your older one needs a snack, a bath, and you, all at the same time. Going from one kid to two is a big jump, and it's normal to feel stretched thin. It does settle, and you'll find your rhythm.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Why it can feel harder

  • No more tag-team. With one kid, someone could rest. With two, everyone's on duty.
  • Your older child needs you more, just when the baby needs everyone.
  • Big feelings from the big kid. Tantrums, clinginess, or acting younger are common after a new baby arrives. If it has turned into hitting or pushing the baby, see when your older kid acts out.
  • Less sleep, less space, and less time for yourself than ever.

What helps

  • Become the big kid's person. Many dads take the lead with the older child while the baby is tiny. Bedtime, breakfast, or park trips with Dad can become something special.
  • Ten minutes of one-on-one time a day with your older child goes a long way. Let them pick what you do.
  • Give the big kid a job. Fetching a diaper or choosing the baby's socks helps them feel part of it.
  • Lower the bar. Frozen dinners, more screen time for a few weeks, a messy house. It's survival season.
  • Say yes to help. If someone offers to take the older child for an afternoon, take it.
  • Find a pocket of time for you. A walk, a workout, a quiet coffee. Even short breaks add up.

When there’s no break anywhere in the week

With one kid there were gaps. Somebody could take a nap, somebody could go out for an hour. With two, a lot of dads find the gaps have simply gone, and that is a different problem from being tired. Tired gets fixed by sleep. This one is about there being no point in the week that is actually yours.

It shows up hardest if you work from home. The kids are audible through the wall all day while you are supposed to be producing, and the second you shut the laptop you are on duty, so the day never has an edge in it. Then the weekend arrives and there is no sleeping in, because somebody is awake at six either way.

The thing that catches dads out is counting the wrong hours as rest. Taking one kid swimming, doing the bedtime run, driving somebody to a class: you are out of the house, but you are still the adult in charge. Being out is not the same as being off. If every hour away from work has a child attached to it, the honest answer is that you have had no break this week, even though it felt like you were doing things.

What actually helps, in rough order of how likely it is to happen:

  • Put one thing in the week that has no kid in it. Ninety minutes, same day each week, booked like a dentist appointment. Protected and repeating beats long and hoped-for.
  • Build an edge into the end of the workday. Ten minutes between logging off and walking in. Sit in the car, walk round the block, anything that marks the switch. Dads who do this say it is the cheapest change they made.
  • Trade shifts instead of hoping for time. You take both kids Saturday morning, she takes both Sunday morning, and both of you actually get off duty. Vague goodwill tends to turn into neither of you getting anything.
  • Take the offers. If a grandparent or a friend offers to take one kid for an afternoon, say yes even if the house is a mess and you feel like you should decline.
  • Count the solo-kid trips as what they are. They are good for your kid and good for the two of you, and they are not rest. Keep doing them, and get your break somewhere else.

If this has been your life for months, keep an eye on where it is going. Running with no break for long enough looks a lot like a short fuse, numbness and dreading the evening, and in fathers that is usually what depression looks like rather than crying. There is more in worn down as a new dad, and if your workday is part of it, working from home with a newborn.

Looking after yourself

Dads can struggle after any baby, not just the first. If you're feeling worn out, short-tempered or numb for more than a couple of weeks, read worn down as a new dad or the signs of postpartum depression in dads. Talking with other fathers helps too: PSI's free dads' groups meet online, so there's no babysitter needed.

Common questions

Is going from one kid to two harder?

For many families, yes. With two children there's less chance to rest, and the older child often needs more attention just as the baby arrives. It usually settles as routines form.

How can dads help an older child adjust to a new baby?

Take the lead with the older child, give them ten minutes of one-on-one time a day, and give them small jobs that help them feel included.

Can dads get postpartum depression after a second baby?

Yes. Fathers can struggle after any birth, not only the first. If symptoms last more than two weeks, talk to a doctor.

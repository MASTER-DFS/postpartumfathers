When you had anxiety or depression before the baby

Maybe you’ve been through it before, or you’re on medicine for it now. A new baby can stir it back up. It doesn’t have to. Knowing your own signs is a real head start.

If you’re thinking about suicide, please call or text 988 any time, day or night. If you’re in danger right now, call 911.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Try this tonight

  • Take your medicine if you’re on any. Set a daily alarm on your phone.
  • Write down your early signs, the things that showed up first last time.
  • Text one person who knows your history: “Keep an eye on me these next few months.”

Why the first year can shake things loose

If you’ve dealt with anxiety or depression before, a new baby can stir it back up. Less sleep, more worry and less time for the things that kept you steady all add up.

A history of anxiety or depression is one of the known risk factors for postpartum depression in dads. It may never come back, but it’s worth having a plan.

Here’s the good news. You already know what your bad days look like. Most new dads don’t. That gives you a head start.

Know your early signs

Think back to the last time things got hard. What showed up first? Write it down, or tell someone you trust. Common early signs:

  • Sleep gets worse even when the baby sleeps
  • Worry that loops and won’t switch off
  • Pulling away from people, or going quiet
  • A shorter fuse than usual
  • Drinking more, or scrolling for hours to numb out
  • Feeling flat, or like nothing matters

If two or three of these show up for more than a week or two, that’s your cue to act early, not wait.

If you take medicine

  • Don’t stop or change it on your own. Stopping suddenly can bring symptoms back or cause withdrawal.
  • Keep taking it through the chaos. Set a phone alarm. Missed doses are easy when every day blurs together.
  • Tell whoever prescribes it about the baby. They may want to check in more often for a while.

Your old tools may stop working

The gym, a long run, a full night’s sleep, time alone. A lot of what kept you steady may be gone for now. If your old routine doesn’t fit right now, scale it down instead of dropping it.

  • Ten minutes instead of an hour. A walk with the stroller counts.
  • Protect one block of sleep. Sleep loss is one of the biggest triggers. Splitting the nights helps.
  • Keep one thing that’s yours, even if it’s small. See no time to yourself.
  • Book a check-in with your old therapist or doctor, even if you feel fine. It’s easier to keep a door open than to reopen it.

Tell one person

Pick one person who knows your history. Your partner, a friend, your brother. Tell them what your early signs look like and ask them to say something if they notice. You don’t have to explain everything.

If it comes back

It’s not your fault, and it doesn’t mean you’re a bad dad. It’s a health problem that showed up at a hard time. You’ve gotten through it before. Treatment works, and the sooner you start, the quicker it usually lifts.

See how to talk to your doctor and finding a therapist. Dads who have been down this road before can help too. PSI’s free dads’ groups meet online, and our message board is open any time.

If it gets dark

If you’re thinking about suicide, or that your family would be better off without you, call or text 988. It’s free, 24/7, and you don’t have to be in crisis to call. If you’re in danger right now, call 911.

Common questions

Will my anxiety or depression come back after the baby?

Not always. But a history of anxiety or depression is one of the known risk factors for postpartum depression in dads, so it's worth having a plan: know your early signs, protect your sleep, and keep a door open with your doctor or therapist.

Should I stop my medication when the baby comes?

Don't stop or change it on your own. Stopping suddenly can bring symptoms back or cause withdrawal. Talk to whoever prescribes it.

What are the early signs my depression is coming back?

Common ones are worse sleep, worry that won't switch off, pulling away from people, a shorter fuse, drinking or scrolling more, and feeling flat. If a few show up for more than a week or two, talk to your doctor early.

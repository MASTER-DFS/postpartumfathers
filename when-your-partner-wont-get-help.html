Supporting your partner

When your wife has postpartum depression and won't get help

You can see she's struggling. You've maybe even said something. And she says she's fine, or she's too tired, or she'll deal with it later. That's one of the hardest spots a dad can be in. You can't make her get help, but you can make it a lot easier for her to say yes.

If you're ever worried about her safety, you don't have to handle it alone. Call or text 988 any time, day or night. If she talks about hurting herself or the baby, seems confused or out of touch with what's real, sees or hears things that aren't there, or hasn't slept in days, get help right away: call or text 988, or 911 if anyone is in danger right now. More crisis help.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Try this tonight

  • Don't push tonight. Take the baby so she can sleep. Rest makes the next talk easier.
  • Call for advice yourself. The Maternal Mental Health Hotline, 1-833-852-6262, takes calls and texts from partners, 24/7.
  • Plan one calm moment in the next day or two to say what you've noticed.

Why she might be saying no

It usually isn't stubbornness. Many new moms are scared of what asking for help would mean. Common worries:

  • "People will think I'm a bad mom."
  • "If I tell a doctor, they might take the baby."
  • "Every new mom feels like this. It'll pass."
  • "I don't have the energy to make calls or go to appointments."
  • "I don't want medication," or "we can't afford it."

Depression itself can make reaching out feel pointless. Knowing what's behind the no helps you answer the real worry, gently.

How to bring it up

  • Pick a calm moment, not in the middle of a hard night or an argument.
  • Say what you've seen, not a diagnosis. "You've been crying a lot and not sleeping, even when the baby does. I'm worried about you."
  • Lead with love. "You're a good mom. This isn't your fault. I'm on your side."
  • Listen more than you talk. Let her tell you what it feels like, without fixing it.
  • Tell her it's common and treatable. Postpartum depression is one of the most common problems after birth, and most moms get better with help.

What not to say

  • "Snap out of it," or "You should be happy."
  • "What's wrong with you?"
  • "My mom never needed help," or comparing her to other moms.
  • Ultimatums or threats. They make it feel like a fight she has to win.

If a talk goes badly, that's okay. Say sorry for your part, and try again another day.

Make the first step small

"Go see a therapist" can feel like a mountain. A smaller ask is easier to say yes to. Offer to do the hard parts yourself: find the number, make the call, drive, and watch the baby.

  • A regular checkup. Her OB, midwife or family doctor already expects to ask about mood after a birth.
  • The baby's checkup. Many pediatricians ask moms a few questions about mood at well-baby visits. She can answer honestly there.
  • A phone call, not an appointment. The Maternal Mental Health Hotline, 1-833-852-6262, is free, 24/7, and she can call or text.
  • Other moms first. Postpartum Support International runs free online support groups for moms. Hearing "me too" can make the next step feel possible.
  • Someone she trusts. A close friend, sister or mom who has been through it may get further than you can.

If medication is the worry, she can say so. Talk therapy and support groups help too, and she can ask her doctor what's safe while breastfeeding.

If she still says no

Keep the door open. You might say, "Okay. I'm not going anywhere. Can we check in again on Sunday?" Then keep doing the things that help: protect her sleep, take things off her plate, and keep her company.

Write down what you notice and when. If things get worse, or don't get better in a couple of weeks, bring it up again. Many moms say yes on the second or third gentle ask.

You can also get advice without her. Call the hotline above, or the Postpartum Support International HelpLine at 1-800-944-4773. The PSI HelpLine is not a crisis line and not instant. Leave a message or text the word Help, and a trained volunteer gets back to you with support and local options.

If you called her doctor and nothing came of it

Some dads do the brave thing and call her doctor's office themselves. Then nothing happens. No call back, no follow-up, or a polite answer that they can't discuss her care. It can leave you feeling shut out and alone with it. That's common, and it isn't the end of the road.

  • They can listen even when they can't share. Privacy rules limit what the office can tell you, not what you can tell them. Ask the nurse to note your concerns in her chart.
  • Be specific. "She hasn't slept in a week even when the baby sleeps, and she's crying most days." Concrete details get taken more seriously.
  • Ask them to bring it up at her next visit, and ask when that is.
  • Try another door. Many pediatricians ask moms about mood at the baby's checkups, and the hotline below can talk it through with you tonight.

If you're worried about her safety, don't wait on a call back. Go straight to the steps below.

When it can't wait

An adult can't be made to get help in most cases. Safety is different. Act now, even if she says no:

  • If she talks about dying, says everyone would be better off without her, or not wanting to be here, call or text 988 together.
  • If she has a plan, or you think she or the baby is in danger, call 911. Don't leave her alone.
  • If she seems confused, sees or hears things that aren't there, or hasn't slept in days, call 911. These can be signs of postpartum psychosis, a medical emergency that is treatable.

Scary, unwanted thoughts about the baby that horrify her are different, and common. Help her call her doctor or the hotline the same day. There's more on that in helping your partner with postpartum depression.

Look after yourself too

Carrying the house and the worry at the same time wears a dad down fast. You can't make her choices for her, and that's not your failure. Keep yourself fed, sleep in shifts, and tell one person what's going on.

When there seems to be nothing for you

Many dads go looking for help for themselves and find that almost everything is written for moms. The forms go to her, the groups are for her, and the dad is left in the waiting room. You're not imagining it. But there is help for you, even if she isn't ready for hers:

  • The Maternal Mental Health Hotline, 1-833-852-6262, takes calls and texts from dads and partners, free and 24/7.
  • The PSI HelpLine, 1-800-944-4773, connects dads and partners with support and local providers. It's not a crisis line.
  • Your own doctor. You can go in about how you're doing. Here's how to bring it up.
  • Other dads. See free support groups for dads.

Dads whose partners have postpartum depression are more likely to struggle too. Watch for the signs in dads. PSI's free dads' groups meet online, and our message board is open any time.

This is a hard season, not forever. With time and the right help, most families come through it.

Common questions

What do I do if my wife has postpartum depression and won't get help?

Pick a calm moment, say what you have noticed without blame, and listen. Offer one small step, like a checkup or a call to the Maternal Mental Health Hotline at 1-833-852-6262, and offer to do it with her. If she says no, keep the door open and ask again in a few days.

Why won't she get help for postpartum depression?

Many moms are afraid of being seen as a bad mother, worry their baby could be taken away, think it will pass, or are too exhausted to make a call. Postpartum depression is common and treatable, and asking for help is what good parents do.

Can I call a hotline for my wife?

Yes. The Maternal Mental Health Hotline at 1-833-852-6262 takes calls and texts from partners, free and 24/7. The Postpartum Support International HelpLine at 1-800-944-4773 helps partners find support and local providers, but it is not a crisis line.

Where can I get support for myself as a dad?

The Maternal Mental Health Hotline at 1-833-852-6262 takes calls and texts from dads and partners, free and 24/7. The PSI HelpLine at 1-800-944-4773 connects partners with support and free online dads' groups. Your own doctor can help too.

When is it an emergency?

Call 911 if she talks about harming herself or the baby and has a plan, seems confused or out of touch with reality, sees or hears things that aren't there, or hasn't slept in days. If she talks about dying or being better off gone, call or text 988 together. Don't leave her alone.

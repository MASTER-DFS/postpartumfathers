When the baby gets hurt

When the baby is choking

It happens in a second. Something goes in the mouth, and suddenly the baby can’t breathe. Most of the time a baby coughs it out on their own. When they can’t, a few steps you can learn in five minutes can clear it. Read this once now, when things are calm, so it’s there if you ever need it.

Rough night? Call or text 988, or 1-833-852-6262 (24/7, dads too).

Right now

  • Coughing hard or crying? Let them cough. Stay close and watch.
  • Can’t cry, cough or breathe? Have someone call 911. Then 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts, again and again.
  • Goes limp? Call 911 on speaker and start infant CPR. The dispatcher will guide you.

Gagging or choking?

Babies gag a lot, especially when they start solid food. Gagging is loud: coughing, sputtering, red face, maybe throwing up a little. It’s the body doing its job. If the baby is coughing hard or crying, let them cough. Stay right there and watch. Don’t pat their back or reach into their mouth, because that can push the object further in.

It’s choking, and you need to act now, if the baby:

  • Can’t cry, cough or make much sound
  • Has a weak, quiet cough that isn’t working
  • Makes a high-pitched sound or squeak when breathing in
  • Is struggling to breathe, with the chest and ribs pulling in
  • Is turning blue or gray around the lips or face

A baby under 1: back blows and chest thrusts

If anyone else is home, shout for them to call 911 while you start. If you’re alone, start right away.

  1. Lay the baby face down along your forearm, resting your arm on your thigh. Hold their jaw in your hand to support the head, and keep the head lower than the body.
  2. Give up to 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your other hand.
  3. Turn the baby face up along your other arm, still supporting the head, head lower than the body.
  4. Give up to 5 quick chest thrusts: the heel of one hand on the middle of the chest, just below the nipple line, pushing straight down.
  5. Keep going, 5 back blows then 5 chest thrusts, until the object comes out or the baby can cry or cough hard.

Don’t do belly thrusts on a baby under 1. Only take the object out of the mouth if you can see it and pick it out easily. Never sweep a finger around blindly.

If the baby stops responding

If the baby goes limp or stops breathing, call 911 now if no one has, and put the phone on speaker. Lay the baby on a firm, flat surface and start infant CPR. The 911 dispatcher will talk you through every step, so you don’t have to remember it all. Look in the mouth before each breath and take out anything you can see.

An older child: back blows and belly thrusts

For a child over 1 who can’t breathe, cough or talk, the steps are a little different.

  1. Kneel or stand behind them and lean them forward a little.
  2. Give 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
  3. Give 5 belly thrusts: wrap your arms around them, make a fist just above the belly button, cover it with your other hand, and pull in and up, hard and fast.
  4. Keep going, 5 and 5, until the object comes out or they can breathe, cough or cry. If they stop responding, call 911 and start CPR.

After it’s out

Call your pediatrician, even if the baby seems completely fine. Back blows and chest thrusts can cause injury, and a small piece can still be stuck lower down. Go to the ER or call 911 if the baby has trouble breathing, keeps coughing or wheezing, drools and can’t swallow, or just doesn’t seem right in the hours after.

Common things babies choke on

  • Food: whole grapes, hot dogs in rounds, nuts, popcorn, hard candy, raw carrot or apple, big chunks of meat or cheese. Cut food small and soft, and quarter grapes the long way.
  • Small things: coins, button batteries, magnets, buttons, beads, small toy parts, marker caps, stickers and bandages.
  • The bandage after shots. Babies can peel it off and put it in their mouth. Keep an eye on it, and take it off once the spot has stopped bleeding.
  • Balloons, popped or not. Keep them away from kids under 3.
  • Your older kid’s toys. If a piece fits through a toilet paper tube, it’s small enough to choke on.

Stay with babies and toddlers while they eat, keep them sitting up, and don’t let them crawl or run around with food. If a baby may have swallowed a button battery or a magnet, even if they seem fine, go to the ER right away and call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 on the way.

Take a class, if you can

Reading this helps. Practicing it once with your hands helps a lot more. Many hospitals, fire departments and community centers run infant CPR and choking classes, and some are free. Ask your pediatrician’s office or the hospital where your baby was born. It’s a couple of hours, and it’s one of the most useful things a new dad can do.

Afterwards, for you

A choking scare can stay with you for days. You might keep replaying it, or feel shaky every time the baby coughs. That’s normal, and it usually fades. If you got the object out, you did the hardest part right.

If the fear doesn’t settle, and you can’t stop checking or can’t sleep, that counts too. See anxious and on edge.

If it gets dark

If you’re running on empty and things feel too heavy, call or text 988, or the Maternal Mental Health Hotline at 1-833-852-6262. Both are free, 24/7, and dads can call. If anyone is in danger right now, call 911.

Common questions

What do I do if my baby is choking?

If the baby is coughing hard or crying, let them cough and watch closely. If they can't cry, cough or breathe, have someone call 911, then give up to 5 back blows and up to 5 chest thrusts, and keep repeating until the object comes out. If the baby stops responding, start infant CPR and the 911 dispatcher will guide you.

How do I tell gagging from choking?

Gagging is loud: coughing, sputtering, a red face. It is normal, especially with new foods. Choking is often quiet: the baby can't cry or cough, makes a squeaky sound breathing in, or turns blue. Choking needs action right away.

Should I see a doctor after my baby choked?

Yes. Call your pediatrician even if the baby seems fine afterwards. Go to the ER or call 911 if the baby has trouble breathing, keeps coughing or wheezing, can't swallow, or doesn't seem right in the hours after.

Can I do the Heimlich on a baby?

Not on a baby under 1. For babies, use back blows and chest thrusts. Belly thrusts, also called the Heimlich, are for children over 1 and adults, alternated with back blows.
